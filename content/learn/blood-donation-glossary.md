Blood banking has its own vocabulary, most of it borrowed from laboratory
medicine, and it gets used around donors and families who have never heard it
before. This page defines the terms you are most likely to encounter, in the
order of how often you will hear them rather than alphabetically.

## Whole blood

Blood as it is collected from a donor, before anything is done to it. A standard
whole-blood donation is a single unit of around 450 millilitres.

Most whole blood is not transfused as whole blood. It is separated into
components within hours of collection, because different patients need different
parts of it and each part keeps best under different conditions. When whole blood
*is* transfused, it must be the patient's own group — the more permissive
compatibility rules people remember apply to separated red cells, not to whole
blood.

## Component

One of the products separated out of a unit of whole blood. The four you will
hear named:

- **Packed red cells**, sometimes PRBC — the oxygen-carrying part, used for blood
  loss and severe anaemia. Refrigerated, and the longest-lasting of the cellular
  products at about 35 days.
- **Platelets** — the fragments that make blood clot. Stored at room temperature
  under continuous gentle agitation, and good for only about 5 days, which is why
  platelets are perpetually in short supply.
- **Fresh frozen plasma**, or FFP — the liquid part, carrying clotting factors.
  Deep frozen, and keeps about a year.
- **Cryoprecipitate** — a concentrate separated from thawed plasma, used for
  specific clotting problems. Also deep frozen, also about a year.

A single donation can therefore help more than one patient, which is the honest
answer to "does one unit really matter".

## Unit, and bag

A unit is one donation's worth of a component. A bag is the physical container it
is in. People use the words interchangeably, and a request for "two units" means
two bags of whatever component was asked for.

## Component separation

The process of spinning collected whole blood in a centrifuge so that it settles
into layers by density, then drawing the layers off into separate bags. It
happens at the blood bank, not at the camp, and it is why the unit you donate in
the morning is not available to a patient in the afternoon.

## Apheresis

A collection method where blood is drawn from a donor, passed through a machine
that removes only one component, and the rest returned to the donor in the same
sitting. It takes longer than a whole-blood donation and needs specific
equipment.

Its main use is platelets. An apheresis platelet donation — you may hear it called
SDP, for single donor platelets — yields enough platelets for a patient from one
donor, rather than pooling the small platelet layers from several whole-blood
donations. For a patient needing platelets repeatedly, fewer donors means less
exposure.

Because the red cells go back to the donor, the interval before a platelet donor
can give again is much shorter than after a whole-blood donation. The blood bank
sets it.

## Screening, and deferral

**Screening** is the medical assessment a donor goes through before collection: a
short history, pulse and blood pressure, and a haemoglobin check. It protects the
donor as much as the recipient.

**Deferral** is the outcome when someone should not donate. A *temporary*
deferral has an end date — recent illness, a recent vaccination, a course of
antibiotics, a tattoo, a recent surgery. A *permanent* deferral means this person
should not donate again.

Being deferred is routine. It happens to people every day at every blood bank,
and it is not a statement about anyone's health in general. The only person who
can decide it is the doctor doing the screening: an online questionnaire — on
Raktify or anywhere else — is a filter to save you a wasted trip, never a
clearance.

## Minimum interval between donations

The waiting period a donor must observe after donating before giving again, so
that the body has replaced what was taken. It differs by what was donated and, for
whole blood, between men and women.

Raktify tracks it so that you are not asked for blood when you are not yet
eligible, and the specific figures belong to the blood bank's own protocol rather
than to a web page.

## TTI screening

Testing for transfusion-transmissible infections. Every unit collected in India
must be tested before release, and the mandatory panel covers HIV, hepatitis B,
hepatitis C, syphilis and malaria.

A unit that tests reactive is discarded and the donor is informed and counselled
privately. This is one of the strongest arguments for donating through a licensed
blood bank: the testing is not optional and not skipped.

## Reactive

The laboratory's word for a test that has come back positive, used in preference
to "positive" because a screening test needs confirmation. A reactive screening
result is a reason to discard the unit and to talk to the donor; it is not by
itself a diagnosis.

## Crossmatch

A laboratory test that mixes a sample of the specific patient's blood with the
specific unit intended for them, to confirm they are compatible in practice and
not just on paper. Required for red cells and whole blood.

Crossmatching is why a hospital cannot simply be handed a compatible-looking unit
and why "we have your group in stock" is not the last step. Platelets and plasma
are handled differently.

## Group, and verified group

Your ABO group and Rh type — A, B, AB or O, positive or negative. Established by
testing a sample, which is something only a laboratory can do.

Raktify distinguishes a **self-reported** group, which you type in and which is
shown marked unverified, from a **verified** group recorded by a licensed blood
bank. Only the verified one is used to match you to a patient. A group remembered
from an old report is wrong often enough that trusting it would eventually send a
family the wrong donor.

## Lookback

The process of tracing, after the fact, where every unit from a particular donor
went — usually because that donor was later found to have an infection that was
not detectable when they donated.

Lookback is the reason blood banking is so insistent about records. It only works
if the chain from donor to unit to patient is complete and cannot be quietly
altered after the fact. Raktify's audit log is append-only for the same reason:
no user, administrator or engineer can edit or delete an entry.

## Chain of custody

The record of where a specific bag has been: issued by the blood bank against a
specific request, received by the hospital, then transfused, returned or written
off. Each step is recorded with who did it and when.

## Camp

A blood donation drive held away from the blood bank's own premises — in a
college, an office, a housing society, a temple. The blood bank brings staff and
equipment; the organiser provides the venue, the people and the logistics.

The organiser never handles blood or makes medical decisions. Collected units go
back to the blood bank's licensed storage for separation and testing like any
other donation.

## Replacement donation

The practice of asking a patient's family to bring donors to offset the units the
patient uses. It is widespread in India and it is not the same as voluntary
donation: the donor is under family pressure, which is exactly the circumstance in
which someone hides a reason they should not be donating.

Voluntary non-remunerated donation — no payment, no family obligation — is what
blood services worldwide work towards, and it is the only kind Raktify supports.

## Voluntary non-remunerated donor

Someone who donates of their own free will, for no payment and under no pressure.
In India, paying a blood donor is illegal. The hospital charge a patient's family
sees covers collection, testing and processing, not the blood.
